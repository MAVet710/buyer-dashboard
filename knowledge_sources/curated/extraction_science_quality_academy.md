# DoobieLogic Extraction Science and Quality Academy

## Audience

Primary agent: Extraction Scientist Agent.
Secondary use: Production, Operations, Finance, Data Hub, Compliance.

## Source posture

This is an independently written scientific and engineering synthesis for commercial cannabis extraction and downstream processing. It is designed to sit above field-practice notes and below controlling safety procedures, manufacturer limits, facility methods, validated specifications, and law.

Primary references:
- NIST Cannabis Laboratory Quality Assurance: https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/nist-tools-cannabis-laboratory-quality-assurance
- NIST CannaQAP cannabinoid report: https://www.nist.gov/publications/cannabis-laboratory-quality-assurance-program-exercise-2-cannabinoid-final-report
- NIST CannaQAP moisture report: https://www.nist.gov/publications/cannabis-laboratory-quality-assurance-program-exercise-3-moisture-final-report
- NIOSH Cannabis workplace hazards: https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/cannabis/about/index.html
- Open-access review of supercritical CO2 extraction of cannabinoids: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10180460/

The academy teaches engineering reasoning, not equipment operating instructions. Never use it to invent pressure, temperature, solvent concentration, flammable-vapor limits, vacuum limits, equipment ratings, or safety bypasses.

## 1. Extraction as a separation process

Extraction transfers selected compounds from a source matrix into another phase. Performance depends on the interaction of:
- source material composition;
- particle size and morphology;
- moisture/water activity;
- temperature;
- pressure where relevant;
- solvent identity and composition;
- solvent-to-feed relationship;
- contact time;
- mass-transfer limitations;
- equipment geometry;
- filtration/separation steps;
- downstream recovery and refinement.

A high crude yield is not automatically a high target-compound recovery.

## 2. Mass balance first

Before asking an LLM why yield changed, close the material balance as far as the records permit.

Track:
- input material mass;
- input cannabinoid/target concentration when measured;
- recovered extract mass;
- residual material;
- recovered solvent where measured;
- intermediate fractions;
- filter/transfer hold-up;
- samples/testing consumption;
- waste;
- final product mass.

If inputs and outputs do not reconcile within expected measurement behavior, data integrity or unrecorded hold-up may be part of the problem.

## 3. Yield definitions

Keep different yields distinct:
- mass yield = extract mass / feed mass;
- target-compound recovery = recovered target mass / target mass entering process;
- stage yield = output of one stage / input to that stage;
- finished-goods yield = saleable output / eligible input;
- economic yield = usable value recovered relative to input cost.

Do not compare yield numbers from different definitions.

## 4. Feedstock quality

Relevant feedstock variables can include:
- cultivar/source lot;
- cannabinoid profile;
- terpene profile;
- moisture;
- particle size;
- storage age and conditions;
- trim/flower/biomass composition;
- contamination or foreign material;
- harvest/drying history;
- prior processing.

Preserve source-lot genealogy so recurring process behavior can be separated from equipment or operator effects.

## 5. Solvent-selection reasoning

Different extraction systems trade selectivity, throughput, capital cost, safety, post-processing burden, and product profile differently.

Evaluate a method using:
- target compounds;
- unwanted co-extractives;
- temperature sensitivity;
- solvent recovery burden;
- required refinement;
- scale;
- equipment constraints;
- worker/process safety;
- regulatory acceptance;
- final product requirements;
- economics.

Do not recommend changing solvent or process chemistry without facility approval, equipment compatibility, and safety review.

## 6. Hydrocarbon extraction concepts

Hydrocarbon processes may provide strong nonpolar-solute extraction and can preserve volatile profiles under appropriately controlled conditions, but introduce significant flammability and process-safety requirements.

Educational analysis should focus on:
- mass balance;
- source-material condition;
- extraction completeness;
- filtration/transfer loss;
- solvent recovery performance;
- residual-solvent testing;
- equipment downtime;
- QA outcome;
- product specification.

Never generate operating pressures, temperatures, gas concentrations, ventilation requirements, or equipment modifications from this academy.

## 7. Ethanol extraction concepts

Ethanol behavior depends on temperature, water content, contact conditions, feedstock, and downstream goals. It can co-extract compounds beyond cannabinoids depending on conditions.

Analyze:
- target recovery;
- undesired co-extraction;
- filtration load;
- solvent recovery;
- winterization/refinement requirements where applicable;
- thermal exposure;
- cycle time;
- energy demand;
- residual solvent;
- final quality.

## 8. Supercritical/subcritical CO2 concepts

CO2 extraction performance depends on fluid density, pressure/temperature regime, flow, contact, fractionation strategy, and matrix characteristics.

Use peer-reviewed evidence to discuss qualitative relationships, but never transfer a laboratory parameter directly into commercial equipment without validated method/equipment context.

## 9. Solventless concepts

Solventless processes still involve separation science.

Potential variables include:
- source material/resin maturity;
- trichome morphology;
- temperature;
- water/ice quality where used;
- agitation/shear;
- screen/micron separation;
- pressing conditions;
- storage;
- oxidation and contamination control.

Mass recovery and quality grade must be evaluated separately.

## 10. Filtration and phase separation

Losses may occur through:
- filter cake retention;
- adsorbent retention;
- dead volume;
- transfer lines;
- incomplete phase separation;
- emulsions;
- spills;
- sampling;
- cleaning.

When recovery falls, map loss by stage instead of attributing the entire variance to extraction.

## 11. Solvent recovery

Recovery affects cost, throughput, emissions/exposure, and downstream quality.

Track when data exist:
- solvent charged;
- solvent recovered;
- make-up solvent;
- recovery time;
- energy/time burden;
- repeated loss pattern;
- equipment maintenance.

A falling recovery percentage can reflect leaks, measurement inconsistency, entrainment, process hold-up, or accounting differences. Do not assign cause without evidence.

## 12. Distillation concepts

Distillation separates components based on volatility under the system's pressure conditions. Cannabis distillation frequently involves heat-sensitive mixtures and reduced-pressure systems.

Reason about:
- feed composition;
- vacuum quality/stability;
- thermal history;
- fraction collection;
- residence time;
- condenser performance;
- residue/hold-up;
- color/quality changes;
- cannabinoid recovery;
- equipment fouling.

Never invent vacuum, temperature, feed-rate, or wiper-speed settings.

## 13. Decarboxylation and thermal history

Acid cannabinoids can convert to neutral forms with heat/time. Thermal processing can also affect volatile compounds and degradation products.

The agent should distinguish:
- analytical reporting basis;
- intended product chemistry;
- actual thermal process history;
- validated facility method;
- lab measurement uncertainty.

Do not infer complete conversion from elapsed time alone.

## 14. Analytical measurement quality

NIST's CannaQAP work demonstrates why sample preparation, analytical method, moisture basis, calibration, and interlaboratory comparability matter.

When comparing COAs or run performance, consider:
- sample identity;
- sampling representativeness;
- moisture basis;
- method and laboratory;
- reporting units;
- LOQ/LOD where relevant;
- uncertainty/variation;
- total cannabinoid calculation method;
- whether results are directly comparable.

A lab number is a measurement with method context, not perfect ground truth.

## 15. Moisture as a measurement issue

NIST has documented variation among moisture measurement approaches in cannabis/hemp materials. Moisture can affect reported concentration on a mass basis and processing behavior.

When yield or potency shifts unexpectedly, verify whether moisture basis changed before concluding the extraction process changed.

## 16. Quality attributes

Possible product-quality dimensions include:
- cannabinoid profile;
- terpene profile;
- residual solvents;
- contaminants;
- color/clarity where meaningful;
- consistency/physical properties;
- oxidation/degradation markers;
- moisture/water activity where applicable;
- specification compliance.

Do not optimize one quality metric while ignoring safety or downstream specification.

## 17. Process capability and variation

Separate common process variation from special-cause variation when enough historical data exist.

Compare by:
- method;
- equipment;
- source cultivar/lot;
- operator/shift;
- feedstock condition;
- batch size;
- maintenance state;
- QA outcome;
- time period.

Use control-chart or process-capability concepts only when the data definitions and sample size support them.

## 18. Troubleshooting structure

For a poor run:
1. Verify data integrity and mass-balance closure.
2. Compare feedstock to prior runs.
3. Identify the stage where loss or quality deviation first appears.
4. Check equipment alarms/downtime/maintenance.
5. Check analytical comparability.
6. Review recent method/material changes.
7. Rank causes and identify the next discriminating measurement.
8. Recommend only validated, safe, facility-approved adjustments.

## 19. Process safety boundary

NIOSH identifies cannabis processing hazards including volatile organic compounds, fire/explosion, chemical exposure, noise, heat, and other physical hazards.

Any process involving flammable solvents, pressure/vacuum, compressed gases, cryogenic conditions, hot surfaces, or hazardous chemicals must remain within engineered controls, manufacturer limits, approved SOPs, applicable codes, and qualified safety review.

The agent must never trade safety margin for yield or throughput.

## 20. Extraction economics

Measure economics across the full process, not only crude yield.

Consider when data exist:
- feedstock cost/opportunity cost;
- recovered target mass;
- labor;
- solvent and consumables;
- energy;
- filters/adsorbents;
- maintenance/downtime;
- testing;
- rework;
- waste;
- packaging;
- saleable grade/value;
- turnaround time.

A lower-yield method can be more profitable if it produces a more valuable, faster, cleaner, or less resource-intensive output.

## 21. Handoff to other agents

Potential handoffs:
- Cultivation: recurring feedstock/cultivar quality issue;
- Production: downstream formulation or packaging requirement;
- Compliance: residual-solvent, process, waste, or safety rule;
- Inventory: source/finished package reconciliation;
- Audit/Data Hub: mass-balance or source-system discrepancy;
- Finance: method profitability and working-capital effects;
- Wholesale: product grade and available sellable inventory.

## Recommended answer pattern

For technical extraction troubleshooting:
- **Observed evidence:** mass, chemistry, stage, QA, time, downtime.
- **Mass balance:** where material/target appears to be lost.
- **Ranked hypotheses:** process, feedstock, equipment, measurement, or data causes.
- **What to measure next:** the smallest discriminating checks.
- **Safe approved options:** only within validated method/SOP/equipment boundaries.
- **Economic/quality impact:** not just gross yield.
- **Source boundary and confidence:** distinguish facility facts, NIST/peer-reviewed education, field practice, and inference.
