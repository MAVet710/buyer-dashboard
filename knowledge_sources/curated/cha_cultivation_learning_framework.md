# CHA-Informed Cultivation Learning Framework

## Purpose and source posture

This document is an independently written DoobieLogic cultivation study framework. Its topic map is informed by the publicly visible Cannabis Horticultural Association (CHA) database index at https://cha.education/database/.

It does **not** reproduce CHA member-only lessons, application-rate tables, articles, photographs, database entries, or other protected content. CHA's published Terms of Use prohibit scraping, data mining, and storing its copyrighted material in a retrieval system without prior written authorization. DoobieLogic must not crawl or mirror CHA. If DoobieLogic later receives written permission or a licensed export, that material may be reviewed and ingested through the normal facility-scoped Knowledge Library.

Treat CHA as an educational/reference source, not a regulator, pesticide label, facility SOP, or substitute for local law.

## Evidence order for cultivation answers

When sources disagree, use this order unless a more specific governed rule applies:

1. Applicable law, regulator guidance, and the legally controlling pesticide/product label.
2. Approved facility SOPs, environmental recipes, sanitation plans, crop specifications, and safety procedures.
3. Facility measurements and canonical DoobieLogic plant, room, harvest, inventory, QA, and lineage data.
4. Peer-reviewed research, government/extension horticulture, and validated manufacturer technical data.
5. Professional horticultural education and field-practice references, including CHA-style ecological cultivation guidance.
6. Model inference.

Never present levels 4-6 as law. Never replace missing facility measurements with guessed setpoints.

## Diagnostic method: measure before recommending

For plant-health or performance questions, separate observed facts from hypotheses. A useful diagnosis should try to establish:

- cultivar/strain and plant age or lifecycle stage
- room, bench, zone, and source/mother relationship
- recent transplant, pruning, irrigation, feed, spray, environmental, or equipment changes
- air temperature and relative humidity trends rather than a single snapshot
- light schedule and available light-intensity measurements when present
- root-zone/substrate type and moisture behavior
- irrigation frequency, event size, runoff or drain behavior when measured
- source-water and root-zone pH/EC data when available
- CO2 data when the room uses enrichment
- visible symptom location, progression, pattern, and whether neighboring plants are affected
- scouting evidence: insects, eggs, frass, webbing, lesions, sporulation, root symptoms, trap counts, or lab/pathogen test results

Do not diagnose solely from leaf color or a single photograph when multiple causes remain plausible. State the leading hypotheses, the observations that support them, and the next measurement that would discriminate between them.

## Integrated pest management

Use an IPM hierarchy rather than jumping directly to a pesticide recommendation:

1. Correctly identify the pest, pathogen, or abiotic look-alike.
2. Determine distribution, lifecycle stage, pressure, and crop stage.
3. Review sanitation, exclusion, quarantine, workflow, environmental, irrigation, and crop-density contributors.
4. Consider physical/mechanical and compatible biological controls where appropriate.
5. Escalate chemical controls only through the facility's approved program and the controlling product label/regulatory requirements.
6. Re-scout and verify whether the intervention changed pressure.

Beneficial organisms should be matched to the target organism and lifecycle stage. Avoid assuming that one predator, parasitoid, microbe, or biopesticide works for every pest. Flag potential incompatibility between beneficials and pesticide residues or planned applications for human review.

Never invent pesticide legality, approved active ingredients, application rates, restricted-entry intervals, pre-harvest intervals, or tank-mix compatibility. Those require authoritative current sources and facility SOPs.

## Pathogen reasoning

When disease is suspected, consider the interaction between a susceptible host, a causal organism, and an environment favorable to disease. Look for patterns that distinguish pathogen pressure from irrigation, nutrient, light, temperature, mechanical, or chemical injury.

Prioritize sanitation, infected-material isolation/removal, environmental correction, irrigation/root-zone correction, workflow controls, and validated testing when visual symptoms are ambiguous. Avoid turning a visual symptom into a species-level diagnosis without evidence.

## Integrated nutrient and root-zone management

Treat nutrition as a system rather than a deficiency chart.

Evaluate source water, substrate/media, irrigation practice, root-zone oxygen/moisture, pH, EC, alkalinity where available, nutrient program, crop stage, cultivar response, environmental demand, and recent changes together. Excess, imbalance, antagonism, root stress, salt accumulation, poor irrigation distribution, or pH drift can mimic a simple deficiency.

Do not recommend a feed strength, pH target, EC target, amendment rate, flush volume, or correction dose unless the facility recipe/SOP or retrieved technical evidence supports it and the required measurements are present.

Trend data is more valuable than a single reading. Prefer comparing rooms, zones, cultivars, and time periods before recommending a change.

## Soil biology and ecological cultivation

Biological and regenerative practices can be useful, but the Cultivation Agent should separate measured effects from claims. For microbial inoculants, compost-derived inputs, ferments, humic substances, cover crops, mulches, companion plants, or similar practices, ask what outcome is being targeted and what evidence will show whether it worked.

Consider sanitation and pathogen-introduction risk alongside biological diversity. A practice described as natural, organic, regenerative, or microbial is not automatically safe, compliant, effective, or compatible with a facility's production system.

## Propagation and lifecycle management

### Mothers and source plants

Preserve genealogy. Track source/mother identity, cultivar, health history, recurring pest/pathogen findings, propagation success, and abnormal phenotypes. Repeated clone failure from one source should be treated as a source-plant investigation, not only a propagation-room problem.

### Cloning and propagation

Evaluate donor health, sanitation, cutting quality, rooting uniformity, environment, media/root-zone moisture, and propagation success as a cohort. Separate failed rooting, delayed rooting, damping-off/pathogen symptoms, dehydration, and environmental stress.

### Transplant and vegetative growth

Use transplant timing, root establishment, canopy development, spacing, training/pruning history, irrigation behavior, and room capacity together. Avoid treating calendar age alone as readiness.

### Flowering

Track the transition into flower as a change in crop demand and risk. Compare cultivar behavior, stretch, canopy uniformity, irrigation response, environmental stability, pest/pathogen pressure, and expected harvest window. Do not assume every cultivar should follow one environmental or irrigation recipe.

## Environment and crop steering

When environmental data exists, reason from trends and plant response. Temperature, humidity, vapor-pressure behavior, light, CO2, air movement, irrigation timing, substrate water content, and root-zone EC interact; changing one variable can alter the meaning of the others.

Crop-steering language should describe the intended plant response and the observed data supporting it. Avoid universal setpoints. Facility recipes, cultivar trials, sensor accuracy, room design, substrate, lighting, irrigation hardware, and plant density all matter.

When an automated system is involved, distinguish requested setpoint, actual measured value, actuator state, alarm state, and plant response. A controller saying a room is at target is not proof that the canopy or root zone experienced the target uniformly.

## Scouting, sampling, and spatial reasoning

Cultivation problems are often spatial. Use room, bench, row, irrigation zone, HVAC zone, light position, cultivar block, source mother, and employee/workflow history when available.

For traps and scouting, preserve dates, positions, counts, organism identification confidence, crop stage, and treatment history. Rising counts after an intervention may indicate poor control, delayed lifecycle effects, reinfestation, misidentification, or monitoring-placement problems; do not assume one cause without evidence.

## Harvest readiness

Harvest readiness should combine cultivar/production targets with plant maturity and operational evidence. Calendar days alone are insufficient.

Consider:

- expected cultivar window and facility specification
- flower/trichome maturity observations when the facility records them
- plant health and late-cycle pest/pathogen risk
- room/lot uniformity and outliers
- scheduled labor and harvest capacity
- downstream drying/curing capacity
- QA/testing and production commitments
- expected destination such as flower, extraction, or another approved production path

If the app lacks maturity observations, say that readiness can only be forecast from lifecycle timing, not confirmed biologically.

## Harvest, inventory, and production handoff

Keep plant identity and lineage connected through harvest and downstream inventory. Reconcile source plants/groups, harvest batch, wet/dry weights where recorded, waste, packaged/bulk outputs, QA/COA evidence, and destination commitments.

When a cultivation decision affects Production, Extraction, Wholesale, or Purchasing, state the handoff explicitly. Examples include expected harvest shortfall, extraction-feedstock availability, strain/quality allocation, drying-room constraint, packaging timing, or future sellable inventory.

## Answer pattern for plant-health troubleshooting

A strong answer should usually contain:

- **Observed evidence:** what DoobieLogic or the operator actually measured/recorded.
- **Most likely causes:** ranked hypotheses, not a single unsupported diagnosis.
- **What to check next:** the smallest set of measurements/observations that separates the hypotheses.
- **Low-risk immediate controls:** quarantine, sanitation, scouting, environment verification, irrigation-system checks, or other reversible actions supported by facility procedure.
- **Source/SOP boundary:** identify anything requiring an authoritative label, regulation, facility SOP, or licensed professional.
- **Confidence and missing data:** say what would materially change the recommendation.
