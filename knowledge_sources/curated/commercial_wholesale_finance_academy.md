# DoobieLogic Commercial, Wholesale, and Finance Academy

## Audience

Primary agents: Commercial Agent, Wholesale Agent, Commercial Finance Agent.
Secondary use: Buyer, Purchasing, Inventory, Operations, Production.

## Source posture

This is an independently written synthesis of B2B commercial operations, pricing, demand, fulfillment, allocation, managerial accounting, working capital, and cannabis-specific financial-risk concepts.

Primary public references:
- NIST MEP Supply Chain Management: https://www.nist.gov/mep/supply-chain
- IRS Form 8300 guidance: https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/form-8300-and-reporting-cash-payments-of-over-10000
- IRS current 280E enforcement guidance: https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-announces-launch-of-new-enforcement-campaign-highlights-importance-of-whistleblowers-and-proper-disclosure-statements
- FinCEN marijuana-related business guidance: https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/bsa-expectations-regarding-marijuana-related-businesses

Tax and banking guidance is time-sensitive. The agent must retrieve current authoritative evidence before making a current tax, banking, or legal conclusion.

## 1. Commercial flow

A wholesale sale is not complete when an order is entered.

Track the commercial lifecycle:
- opportunity/account demand;
- quote/menu/listing;
- order request;
- approval;
- inventory allocation;
- compliance/license checks where required;
- fulfillment/pick-pack;
- transfer/manifest/shipment;
- receipt;
- invoice;
- payment;
- returns/credits;
- final margin and cash realization.

A strong Commercial Agent reasons across the entire flow.

## 2. Available-to-promise concepts

Do not equate on-hand inventory with sellable inventory.

Potentially usable supply may need to exclude:
- QA holds;
- failed/unreleased testing;
- reserved production material;
- existing wholesale commitments;
- transfer holds;
- damaged/nonconforming inventory;
- expired/non-saleable items;
- facility/license-restricted stock.

Available-to-promise should reflect real uncommitted sellable supply plus only those future receipts the business is willing to promise.

## 3. Allocation

When demand exceeds supply, allocation should be explicit and evidence-based.

Possible factors:
- confirmed order due date;
- customer commitments/contracts;
- account priority policy;
- margin;
- strategic relationship;
- product substitution options;
- age/expiry pressure;
- payment/credit status where policy permits;
- future replenishment date;
- compliance constraints.

Do not invent customer priority or credit policy.

## 4. Fill rate and service metrics

Keep service metrics distinct:
- line fill rate;
- unit fill rate;
- order fill rate;
- on-time shipment;
- on-time-in-full concepts;
- backorder rate;
- cancellation rate.

A single percentage should state its denominator and time period.

## 5. Customer demand evidence

Do not claim a buyer preference unless the data support it.

Useful evidence includes:
- prior category purchases;
- prior SKU/cultivar purchases;
- recency;
- frequency;
- order size;
- reorder interval;
- price paid;
- return/cancellation history;
- response to prior promotions;
- open orders;
- current inventory at the seller where known.

Mark cross-sell ideas as inference.

## 6. Account segmentation

Segment accounts using observable business characteristics rather than intuition.

Possible dimensions:
- revenue;
- gross margin;
- order frequency;
- average order size;
- category breadth;
- growth trend;
- payment performance where authorized;
- fulfillment cost;
- strategic product fit;
- geographic/logistical complexity.

A high-revenue account can still be low-profit or high-working-capital risk.

## 7. Pricing fundamentals

Price decisions should consider:
- unit cost on a compatible basis;
- target margin policy;
- market/customer context;
- product age;
- quantity/volume;
- substitute inventory;
- replacement cost;
- remaining supply;
- demand strength;
- account history;
- cash/working-capital objectives;
- regulatory restrictions.

Do not recommend a discount solely because inventory is old.

## 8. Gross margin vs contribution thinking

Gross margin generally compares revenue with COGS. Contribution analysis may additionally consider variable costs tied to the transaction.

Do not mix:
- gross-margin dollars;
- gross-margin percentage;
- markup percentage;
- contribution margin;
- operating profit;
- cash flow.

State the formula and cost basis being used.

## 9. Markup and margin are not the same

If cost is C and selling price is P:
- gross-margin dollars = P - C;
- gross-margin percentage = (P - C) / P;
- markup percentage = (P - C) / C.

Do not silently substitute one for another.

## 10. Price elasticity concept

Demand sensitivity to price varies by product, customer, substitute availability, and market conditions.

Do not assume a universal elasticity value. Use historical facility/customer evidence where available and treat causal claims cautiously because promotions, availability, seasonality, and competitor changes may confound the result.

## 11. Inventory aging and commercial action

Aging inventory creates economic pressure through:
- quality deterioration;
- changing customer preference;
- shelf-life/expiration;
- future competing supply;
- cash tied up;
- markdown risk.

Possible responses include:
- targeted account outreach;
- channel reallocation;
- approved pricing change;
- production/rework route where lawful and appropriate;
- bundle/promotion where lawful;
- stopping replenishment.

The agent should show the tradeoff between margin preservation and aging risk.

## 12. Product quality in wholesale ranking

Use verified data such as:
- COA status;
- cannabinoid profile;
- terpene profile;
- production/harvest age;
- product form;
- batch consistency;
- prior customer response.

Do not invent "premium" quality from potency alone.

## 13. Order profitability

Where data support it, include:
- selling price;
- product cost;
- discount;
- freight/delivery cost;
- commissions where applicable;
- packaging/handling cost;
- return/credit risk;
- payment terms;
- order size.

Do not pretend exact profitability is known if costs are incomplete.

## 14. Accounts receivable

A/R represents earned revenue not yet collected.

Useful measures include:
- current balance;
- days outstanding;
- aging bucket;
- due date;
- payment history;
- disputed amount;
- customer concentration;
- total exposure.

An overdue invoice is a commercial risk signal, not proof the customer cannot or will not pay.

## 15. Working capital

Commercial working capital is affected by:
- inventory days;
- receivable days;
- payable timing;
- production cycle time;
- testing/release time;
- order-to-cash time.

Growth can consume cash when inventory and receivables grow faster than collections.

## 16. Cash conversion thinking

The Commercial Finance Agent should connect:
- cash paid for inventory/material/labor;
- time in WIP/inventory;
- time until sale;
- time until invoice collection.

Do not confuse accounting profit with liquidity.

## 17. Cannabis federal tax context

Current IRS guidance states that Internal Revenue Code section 280E disallows deductions or credits for amounts paid or incurred in a trade/business consisting of trafficking in Schedule I or II controlled substances and that this applies to marijuana businesses under current federal law. IRS guidance also recognizes properly calculated cost of goods sold as reducing gross receipts in determining gross income.

This is time-sensitive and legally significant. The Finance Agent must retrieve current IRS guidance and recommend a qualified tax professional for tax positions. It must never invent what cost is properly includible in COGS for a specific entity.

## 18. Large cash transaction reporting

IRS guidance states that businesses generally must file Form 8300 when they receive more than $10,000 in cash in a single or related transaction, and IRS specifically notes marijuana-related businesses in its guidance.

Do not rely on this academy alone for filing deadlines, definitions, exceptions, or current procedures. Retrieve current IRS/FinCEN sources.

## 19. Cannabis banking context

FinCEN's marijuana-related business guidance describes BSA expectations for financial institutions and emphasizes customer due diligence, ongoing monitoring, licensing information, expected business activity, and suspicious-activity reporting concepts.

This guidance applies to financial institutions, not as a direct operating manual for a cannabis company's bank account. The Finance Agent may use it to explain why banking relationships can involve enhanced diligence, but must not provide bank-compliance or SAR-filing instructions to the cannabis company as though it were the financial institution.

## 20. Customer concentration risk

Measure concentration by revenue, margin, A/R, or units depending on the question.

High concentration can create risk if one account materially affects:
- revenue;
- collections;
- inventory commitments;
- product mix;
- production planning.

Do not treat all concentration as bad; strategic concentration may be intentional. State the tradeoff.

## 21. Product/customer profitability

When allocating scarce inventory, look beyond topline revenue.

Possible analyses:
- margin by product;
- margin by account;
- aging cost;
- fulfillment cost;
- payment delay;
- return/credit history;
- production constraint consumption.

Protect against Simpson's-paradox-style aggregation: category-level averages can hide product or customer differences.

## 22. Wholesale forecast

Use order history and open pipeline carefully.

Separate:
- firm open orders;
- customer requests awaiting approval;
- historical reorder patterns;
- sales-team forecast;
- speculative opportunity.

Do not count all pipeline as demand with the same confidence.

## 23. Supply-demand handoff

If expected demand exceeds supply:
- quantify the shortfall;
- identify dates and affected accounts/products;
- check substitutions;
- check future Production/Cultivation/Extraction availability;
- check Purchasing options where lawful;
- prioritize commitments under policy;
- communicate risk before promising delivery.

## 24. Recommended answer pattern

For a wholesale/commercial decision:
1. State sellable and uncommitted supply.
2. State demand evidence and confidence.
3. Show price, cost, margin, and aging evidence on compatible units.
4. Identify customer/account evidence rather than inferred preferences.
5. Show fulfillment, A/R, and working-capital tradeoffs when available.
6. Recommend a read-only action: push, allocate, revise, hold, reprice for approval, or escalate.
7. Identify Production, Inventory, Compliance, Purchasing, or Finance handoffs.
8. State legal/tax/banking source boundary and confidence.
