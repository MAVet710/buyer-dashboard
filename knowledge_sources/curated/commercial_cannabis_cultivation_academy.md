# DoobieLogic Commercial Cannabis Cultivation Academy

## Purpose

This is an independently written, source-grounded commercial cultivation curriculum for the DoobieLogic Cultivation Agent. It synthesizes university extension education and peer-reviewed cannabis research into reusable horticultural concepts without copying restricted courseware or turning research conditions into universal recipes.

The agent should use this curriculum to understand **why** cultivation systems behave as they do, then combine that education with facility measurements, approved SOPs, cultivar history, and current regulatory evidence.

## Evidence-transfer rule

Never treat one cannabis study as universally applicable. For every technical claim or recommendation, preserve as much study context as possible:

- drug-type/high-THC cannabis vs cannabinoid hemp vs fiber/grain hemp
- indoor sole-source lighting vs greenhouse vs outdoor/tunnel production
- cultivar/genotype/chemotype
- seed vs clone vs tissue-culture origin
- mother/stock-plant history
- substrate/media and container volume
- irrigation/fertigation system
- plant density and architecture
- lifecycle stage and photoperiod
- light spectrum/intensity and CO2 context
- temperature, humidity, airflow, and root-zone conditions
- experimental duration and measured endpoint

When transferring evidence to a different production context, lower confidence and state the mismatch.

## Module 1 — Plant botany, physiology, genetics, and development

Learn cannabis as a plant before learning recipes. Core concepts include photosynthesis, respiration, transpiration, stomatal behavior, source-sink relationships, apical dominance, meristems, root growth, vegetative/reproductive transitions, photoperiodism, senescence, secondary metabolism, genotype-by-environment interaction, cannabinoid/terpene biosynthesis, and cultivar-specific responses.

Commercial lesson: calendar age is not a substitute for biological state. Two cultivars in the same room may differ in stretch, water use, nutrient uptake, flowering response, canopy shape, maturity, and disease susceptibility.

## Module 2 — Propagation, mothers, clones, and genetic-health preservation

Treat propagation as both a horticultural process and a biosecurity process.

Understand donor/mother vigor, cutting position and quality, sanitation, rooting physiology, humidity and transpiration control, media moisture/oxygen balance, acclimation/hardening, root uniformity, clone-cohort performance, source-plant genealogy, pathogen testing, and tissue culture/micropropagation.

A repeated clone problem can originate from the mother/source plant, propagation environment, sanitation workflow, rooting media, irrigation, or pathogen load. Preserve source identity so the agent can distinguish those causes.

Cornell propagation education emphasizes healthy mother plants, sanitation, environment, media, humidity, rooting, IPM, and micropropagation as a method for generating large numbers of clean clones under controlled conditions.

Primary source:
- Cornell propagation webinar/resource: https://hemp.cals.cornell.edu/2023/02/06/cornell-hemp-webinar-feb-8-propagation-with-mark-bridgen/

## Module 3 — Controlled-environment physiology, HVAC, airflow, and microclimates

Environment is a coupled system, not separate temperature and RH numbers.

Understand leaf temperature vs air temperature, vapor-pressure behavior, transpiration, stomatal conductance, airflow, boundary layers, canopy microclimates, dew/condensation risk, sensible vs latent HVAC load, dehumidification, CO2, light-driven demand, room recovery after irrigation, and spatial sensor variation.

Commercial lesson: a room controller reporting target does not prove that every leaf or root zone experienced target conditions.

Cornell's 2026 controlled-environment education highlights airflow as a major driver of canopy uniformity, water use, nutrient uptake, and yield, while excessive airflow can create mechanical stress.

Primary source:
- Cornell Controlled Environment webinar: https://hemp.cals.cornell.edu/2026/04/06/cornell-hemp-webinar-apr-8-controlled-environment-with-allison-justice/

## Module 4 — Lighting science and photoperiod

Understand PPFD, PAR, daily light integral, spectrum, fixture distribution, canopy penetration, photon efficacy, photoperiod, photosynthetic light response, lower-canopy shading, and the energy/HVAC cost of additional photons.

Research in indoor drug-type cannabis found that increasing light can increase yield while cannabinoid potency does not necessarily increase in parallel. Therefore, the commercial optimum is an economic and biological tradeoff, not simply the maximum light the hardware can deliver.

Photoperiod research also shows that the traditional 12-hour flowering photoperiod is not universally optimal. Different cultivars can initiate and develop flowers under different daylengths, so cultivar trials matter.

Primary sources:
- Rodriguez-Morrison, Llewellyn & Zheng 2021, indoor light intensity: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2021.646020/full
- Peterswald et al. 2023, flowering photoperiod: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10386198/

## Module 5 — Root-zone science, irrigation, water use, and crop steering

Treat irrigation as plant-water relations plus substrate physics.

Understand substrate water-holding capacity, air-filled porosity, volumetric water content, root-zone oxygen, irrigation uniformity, emitter performance, input vs root-zone EC, salt accumulation, leachate/runoff interpretation, dryback, shot size/frequency, timing, root temperature, water-use trends, and cultivar demand.

Commercial crop-steering language often divides irrigation into morning rehydration/ramp-up, daytime maintenance, and overnight dryback. The agent may use those concepts descriptively, but should not invent shot sizes, target drybacks, EC stacks, or timing recipes. Facility SOPs, substrate choice, plant size, cultivar, sensor calibration, room climate, and trial evidence control the actual program.

Research also shows genotype can matter as much as or more than irrigation stress response. Severe drought should not be assumed to improve quality; newer controlled-environment work found moderate deficit irrigation could reduce water use without the same penalty as severe stress in the tested genotypes.

Primary sources:
- Controlled-environment density/vegetative-duration study with root-zone monitoring: https://www.mdpi.com/2311-7524/12/5/619
- 2026 controlled-environment drought research: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2026.1930650/abstract
- 2024 cannabis cutting production/water-use study: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2024.1371702/full

## Module 6 — Mineral nutrition and deficiency diagnosis

Nutrition is an integrated root-zone system, not a leaf-color lookup table.

Understand essential macro- and micronutrients, nutrient mobility, uptake, antagonism, pH chemistry, EC, alkalinity, source-water chemistry, tissue analysis, nutrient-form effects, developmental demand, root health, and environment-driven uptake.

Controlled deficiency research withholding N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, and Mn documented that visual symptoms and tissue analysis do not always align perfectly. The practical lesson is to diagnose deficiencies using symptoms plus tissue/root-zone/water/irrigation evidence rather than visual appearance alone.

Cannabis research also shows that nutrient quantity and nutrient form can alter yield and secondary metabolism. More fertilizer is not automatically more flower or better chemistry.

Primary sources:
- Cockson et al. 2023, single-element deficiency symptomology: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36771506/
- Saloner & Bernstein 2022, nitrogen-form response: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2022.830224/full
- Cornell nutrient-management education: https://hemp.cals.cornell.edu/2023/02/20/cornell-hemp-webinar-feb-22-indoor-cultivation-and-nutrient-management-with-nirit-bernstein/

## Module 7 — IPM, scouting, beneficials, pathogens, and crop biosecurity

Use prevention and diagnosis before intervention.

Core IPM sequence:
1. prevent introduction and establishment;
2. inspect/quarantine incoming plant material;
3. scout consistently and spatially;
4. identify pests/pathogens accurately;
5. trend pressure over time;
6. correct sanitation, environmental, irrigation, and workflow contributors;
7. use compatible biological/cultural/mechanical controls where appropriate;
8. use chemical controls only through current legal labels, regulator rules, and facility SOPs;
9. re-scout to verify efficacy.

UMass greenhouse IPM education emphasizes prevention, regular monitoring, diagnosis, action thresholds, recordkeeping, and combined biological/cultural/mechanical/chemical controls. Virginia Tech hemp IPM emphasizes quarantine, sanitation, clean propagation stock, and disease prevention.

Cannabis-specific disease literature identifies major commercial concerns including Fusarium and Pythium root disease, Botrytis bud rot, powdery mildew, Hop latent viroid, and post-harvest microbes. Disease interactions can compound risk: 2026 work found HLVd-positive plants could show increased susceptibility to Fusarium and powdery mildew in a genotype-dependent manner.

Primary sources:
- UMass greenhouse IPM: https://www.umass.edu/agriculture-food-environment/greenhouse-floriculture/greenhouse-best-management-practices-bmp-manual/integrated-pest-management
- Virginia Tech hemp IPM: https://spes.vt.edu/content/pubs_ext_vt_edu/en/ENTO/ENTO-349/ENTO-349.html
- Buirs & Punja 2024 integrated cannabis disease management: https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/13/6/786
- Punja 2026 HLVd/pathogen interaction: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07060661.2026.2649769

## Module 8 — Canopy architecture, density, pruning, and uniformity

Optimize the crop at the square-foot/room level, not only the single-plant level.

Understand plant density, branch architecture, pruning, defoliation, lower-canopy removal, trellising, canopy depth, light distribution, airflow, labor, disease risk, yield per plant, yield per area, and chemical uniformity.

Drug-type medical cannabis research found that higher density could reduce yield per plant while increasing yield per area, and that lower inflorescences could have substantially lower cannabinoid concentration under shading. Architecture treatments that improve light penetration can change spatial uniformity.

Commercial lesson: maximize saleable, uniform output per area and labor-hour rather than chasing grams per plant in isolation.

Primary sources:
- Danziger & Bernstein 2022, density and cannabinoid uniformity: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9559401/
- Danziger & Bernstein 2021, architecture and chemical uniformity: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8468686/

## Module 9 — Harvest readiness and maturity

Harvest timing is a biological and operational decision.

Consider cultivar-specific development, flower maturity observations, trichome/flower assessments when used by the facility, disease pressure, chemical testing trends when available, room uniformity, labor capacity, downstream drying capacity, and intended product destination.

Do not equate breeder calendar claims or a fixed flowering week with confirmed maturity. If maturity measurements are missing, report a timing forecast rather than biological certainty.

The 2026 industrial drying study also observed that a one-week harvest difference changed cannabinoid accumulation in the tested outdoor material, reinforcing that harvest timing can materially affect chemistry.

Primary source:
- Industrial drying/harvest timing study: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214786126000422

## Module 10 — Drying, curing, storage, and post-harvest quality

Post-harvest is continuing plant-product chemistry and microbiology, not simply "hang until dry."

Understand moisture migration, water activity, equilibrium moisture, drying kinetics, airflow, temperature/RH, flower density, microbial growth risk, decarboxylation, oxidation, terpene volatility, cannabinoid stability, curing, storage, packaging, and endpoint verification.

Cornell research showed drying and curing methods can alter cannabinoid content and microbial levels. Industrial studies support using measured process behavior rather than calendar duration alone to determine drying endpoint.

Primary sources:
- Baek, Grab & Chen 2025, drying/curing and microbial levels: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39942976/
- 2026 industrial tray vs hang drying: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214786126000422
- 2023 industrial tray-drying process control: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0926669023007331

## Module 11 — Commercial cultivation systems thinking

A commercial grow is a biological production system coupled to people, machines, compliance, inventory, QA, and downstream demand.

The agent should connect cultivation education to:

- room turns and space utilization
- clone/mother capacity
- labor requirements and task timing
- irrigation/HVAC/lighting automation
- sensor reliability and calibration
- pest/pathogen containment by room/zone
- expected wet/dry yield
- drying-room capacity
- testing/QA release
- extraction feedstock planning
- packaged-flower allocation
- wholesale commitments
- production bottlenecks
- energy/water use and operating cost

The question is not only "What grows the biggest plant?" It is "What produces the most consistent, compliant, saleable output with acceptable risk, cost, labor, and resource use?"

## Diagnostic answer standard

For a commercial cultivation question, prefer this sequence:

1. **Facility facts** — current measured/recorded data.
2. **Study/SOP context** — what evidence applies and how closely it matches this facility.
3. **Ranked hypotheses** — plausible biological/operational causes.
4. **Discriminating measurements** — smallest useful next checks.
5. **Low-risk reversible action** — only when supported by SOP/evidence.
6. **Commercial consequence** — yield, timing, labor, QA, downstream inventory, or cost impact.
7. **Confidence and transfer limits** — especially when evidence comes from hemp, greenhouse, outdoor, another cultivar, or another substrate.

## Source hierarchy

Use this precedence:

1. applicable law, regulator guidance, and controlling product labels;
2. approved facility SOPs and validated facility recipes;
3. canonical facility measurements and trial history;
4. peer-reviewed cannabis-specific research;
5. university/extension cannabis and controlled-environment horticulture;
6. validated technical/manufacturer education;
7. professional field practice;
8. model inference.

Research explains biology. It does not legalize a pesticide or override facility SOPs.

## Safety and source boundaries

Do not invent or generalize pesticide legality, product labels, application rates, REI/PHI, tank mixes, nutrient recipes, irrigation recipes, light/CO2/VPD targets, or biological-control compatibility. Retrieve authoritative/facility evidence before making those claims.

Do not copy restricted courseware or subscription/member databases. This curriculum is an original synthesis designed to guide retrieval and reasoning.
